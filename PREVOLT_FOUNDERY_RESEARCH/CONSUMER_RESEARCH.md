## 2. What independent evidence says about Indian energy-drink consumers
| Question | Evidence | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Who drinks them? | Category growth came from affordable ₹20 PET packs reaching young, rural and small-town buyers. Sting is most popular with 15–19-year-olds (S14, S15). Monster says Indians drink about 5 servings a year vs 54 in the US (S29). | Medium (syndicated and company sources) |
| How often? | Among 226 Delhi medical students, 62% had consumed energy drinks, but **75% of consumers drank less than one a week** (S46). | Medium (one college) |
| Where do they buy? | About 90% of those students bought from local pan or cigarette shops (S46). | Medium |
| Why? | The study's literature cites staying awake, peer company, exam performance, taste and experimentation (S46). Medical students' caffeine intake rises in exams, with 38% above 300 mg/day (S47). VBL describes energy drinks as appealing to younger consumers "seeking quick energy and alertness" (S25). | Low–Medium; **no Indian study quantifying the ranking of motives was found** |
| Price sensitivity | Sting's switch from ₹50 cans (2017) to ₹20 PET (2020) unlocked mass growth (S14, S15). Campa is extending ₹10 packs (S12, S38). | Medium–High: price is a dominant driver in the mass market |
| Premium willingness | Red Bull sells at ₹125 per 250 ml (S31) and earned ₹1,251 crore revenue in CY2024 (S28). That shows a premium segment exists, built on **brand**, not ingredients. | Medium |
| Sugar | Varun's India volume is 46% low or no sugar (S25). Zero-sugar energy SKUs exist from Monster and Campa (S16, S34). | Medium (supply-side signal, not demand research) |
| Natural / real fruit | **No India-specific, peer-reviewed or audited evidence was found that consumers will pay more for real fruit in energy drinks.** Market-report claims that "natural/organic" will grow faster (S18, S19) are undisclosed model outputs. FSSAI is restricting "natural/real/100%" claims (S08, S09), which limits how a real-fruit benefit can be communicated. | **Unsupported** |

## 3. The founder's hypotheses: evidence for and against
| Hypothesis | Supporting evidence | Contradicting evidence | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1. Consumers want distinctive flavours with credible functional benefits, without unnecessary sugar | Flavour proliferation by incumbents (Sting Gold, Campa range, 28 BLACK); low-sugar share rising (S25) | Incumbents already offer zero-sugar and fruit flavours cheaply (S33, S36). FSSAI now bars functional "energy" claims (S10). | Partly supported, but **not a gap** |
| H2. Enough people value real fruit to pay a higher price | None found | Mass growth is price-led (S15). Claims are restricted (S08). | **Unsupported; test it** |
| H3. Natural fruit creates meaningful differentiation | Untested | Consumers may not detect small juice percentages; Sting Gold is already "blueberry" at ₹20 | **Unknown; triangle-test it** |
| H4. A three-flavour launch is sensible | Flavour variety drives trial (VBL AR, S25) | Triples R&D, shelf-life and MOQ cost (see `UNIT_ECONOMICS.xlsx`). Each flavour sells fewer units, so stock is at higher risk of expiry. | **Not supported for launch** |
| H5. Blueberry, Lemon and Coconut appeal to the same audience | None | Coconut signals hydration and wellness. Blueberry and lemon signal refreshment and fun. Coconut with caffeine is a positioning conflict (see `PRODUCT_FEASIBILITY.md`). | **Doubtful; test it** |
| H6. Students, young professionals and gym-goers are one target | All are 18–35 urban adults | Motives and price tolerance differ: students are price-sensitive (S15), while gym-goers may compare with pre-workout supplements | **Segment and test separately** |

## 4. What would make people repeat-purchase (hypotheses to test)
1. **Taste they prefer in a blind test** over the drink they currently buy.
2. **Availability chilled where the occasion happens:** gym counter, campus store, office, café, 10-minute delivery.
3. **A price per occasion they accept repeatedly**, not just for a one-off trial.
4. **A clear, compliant reason to choose it** (taste, fruit content, caffeine amount stated on pack), not vague "performance".
5. **Habit anchoring** to one occasion, such as a pre-workout ritual or afternoon slump.

Risk: the most frequent consumers are often the most price-sensitive. If 75% of student drinkers buy less than weekly (S46), repeat economics will be hard.

## 5. Validation plan

### 5.1 In-depth interviews (qualitative)
- **Sample:** 12–15 per segment across 3 segments (adult students 18+, young professionals, gym-goers), so 36–45 in total. Recruit current energy-drink buyers (bought in the last 30 days) and lapsed buyers. This is a qualitative rule of thumb for spotting patterns, **not** a statistically representative sample.
- **Rules:** do not show PREVOLT or mention fruit until the last section. Ask about past behaviour, not hypothetical intentions. No leading words ("healthier", "better", "natural").

**Fifteen non-leading interview questions**
1. Tell me about the last time you bought a caffeinated or "energy" drink. Where were you, and what was going on?
2. What did you buy, and how much did you pay?
3. What else did you consider buying at that moment, including coffee, tea or a soft drink?
4. How did you decide on that particular one?
5. How often have you bought one in the last month? What made those occasions different?
6. Walk me through what you notice on the can or bottle, if anything, before you buy.
7. What do you like about the drink you buy most often? What do you dislike?
8. Have you ever stopped buying a drink you used to buy? What happened?
9. Where do you usually buy it, and what happens if it's not available there?
10. What's the most you have paid for a single can? What was the situation?
11. When you hear "made with fruit juice", what do you expect from the drink? *(Ask only in the final section.)*
12. How do you feel about sweeteners versus sugar in drinks? What have you heard about them?
13. Do you compare caffeine amounts between drinks? How?
14. If the drink you usually buy disappeared tomorrow, what would you buy instead?
15. Who else around you drinks these, and does that affect what you choose?

### 5.2 Blind taste testing
- **Products (coded and unbranded, served at the same chilled temperature from identical cups):** PREVOLT lead flavour; the closest incumbent (e.g. Sting Gold for blueberry, Campa Citrus Kick for lemon); a natural-flavour-only version of PREVOLT (to isolate the value of real fruit).
- **Design:** sequential monadic order, randomised and balanced; water and crackers between samples; 18+ only; caffeine disclosed in the consent form; no more than 2 caffeinated samples per person (each sample ≤100 ml).
- **Measures:** 9-point hedonic liking (overall, flavour, sweetness, aftertaste, carbonation); just-about-right scales (sweetness, sourness, fruitiness); forced preference; open comments.
- **Real-fruit discrimination:** a triangle test (real-fruit vs natural-flavour version), commonly run with about 30–40 assessors. Check ISO 4120 (not reviewed in this research) with the food technologist. **If people can't tell the difference, don't pay for real fruit.**
- **Sample size for preference vs a competitor:** to detect a 60:40 preference at 95% confidence and 80% power you need about **195 people**. A 65:35 preference needs about **85**. These are simple binomial approximations. Budget for 100–200 per flavour tested.

### 5.3 Price-sensitivity testing (stated, so treat with caution)
- **Van Westendorp-style** four questions ("too cheap", "cheap", "expensive", "too expensive") after tasting, n ≥ 100 per segment. Also a **Gabor-Granger** ladder at ₹60 / ₹70 / ₹80 / ₹99 / ₹125.
- **Limitation:** stated willingness to pay usually exceeds real behaviour. Use it only to choose the prices to test in 5.4.

### 5.4 Paid-purchase experiments (behaviour, the evidence that matters)
| Experiment | Design | Metric |
|---|---|---|
| Pop-up stalls at 2–3 gyms and adult campus locations | Sell chilled cans at ₹70 on some days and ₹99 on others, randomised by day; compare with a Red Bull/Monster price board; no discounts | Conversion rate (buyers ÷ people who stopped), units per hour |
| Retail placement test | 10–20 outlets, 4–6 weeks, PREVOLT next to incumbents in the cooler | Units per outlet per week; outlet reorder rate |
| Repeat tracking | UPI receipt or QR registration; WhatsApp reorder link; no discount on repeat | % of buyers who repurchase within 14 and 30 days |
| Pre-order with deposit | Online 6-pack pre-order at full price | Paid pre-orders per 1,000 page visitors |

Use only FSSAI-compliant product. **Do not sell any prototype that hasn't cleared lab testing and labelling.** Until then, run tastings only, free and with consent.

### 5.5 Minimum evidence before committing to commercial production (proposed thresholds, not industry standards)
| Gate | Proposed pass threshold |
|---|---|
| Blind preference vs the closest incumbent | Lead flavour preferred by **≥55%** (n ≥ 150 adults in target segments) **and** mean overall liking ≥ 7.0/9 |
| Value of real fruit | The real-fruit version is discriminated (triangle test significant) **and** preferred; otherwise go natural-flavour |
| Paid conversion | At the chosen MRP, sustained sales at stalls and outlets, with ≥3 cans per outlet per week by week 4 |
| Repeat | **≥25%** of first-time buyers repurchase within 30 days at full price |
| Trade | ≥60% of pilot outlets reorder without incentives |
| Economics | Written quotes show a **positive contribution per can sold** at the chosen MRP after trade margins, wastage and sampling (see `UNIT_ECONOMICS.xlsx`) |
| Compliance | Regulatory opinion confirms the product name, ingredients and label |

If two or more gates fail, stop or pivot (e.g. to a single lemon natural-flavour SKU, a non-caffeinated sparkling drink, or a different channel).
